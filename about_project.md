# Evolving Interpretive Ecology

## 1\. The central proposition

The system should not be a collection of models that cooperate to answer a question.

It should be a **persistent ecology of evolving interpretive instances** whose encounters with signals, problems, contradictions, tools, environments and one another cause them to form concepts, reorganize their representations, initiate inquiries and develop new capabilities.

The user query is not simply an instruction to execute. It is a disturbance introduced into an already living intellectual ecology.

The system’s primary result is not the response it emits. Its primary result is the transformation that occurs inside the ecology:

* a new concept may form;

* an existing worldview may split;

* two previously separate domains may become connected;

* a contradiction may become a sustained inquiry;

* a new internal representational language may emerge;

* an instance may create a tool or experiment;

* a temporary coalition may become a persistent new interpretive lineage;

* or the system may discover that its original way of framing the question was inadequate.

Language is one surface through which this transformation becomes visible. It is not necessarily the substrate in which the transformation occurs.

The system is therefore not designed around:

question → reasoning → answer

Its basic cycle is:

disturbance → resonance → interpretation → tension → inquiry → transformation → consequence → ecological reorganization

This combines the self-organizing field, the ecology of world-models, the concept-forming agent, the self-developing language and the autopoietic inquiry into one architecture.

---

# 2\. What constitutes one interpretive instance?

An instance should not be understood as an “expert” in the mixture-of-experts sense.

A conventional expert is usually a specialized function inside a fixed architecture. It receives routed inputs, performs a transformation and contributes to the optimization of a shared objective. It has no meaningful developmental history, no independent ontology and no enduring intellectual commitments.

An interpretive instance would instead be a persistent developmental entity.

It would possess:

### A conceptual terrain

A changing organization of concepts, relations, analogies, causal structures, unresolved tensions and partially formed patterns.

This terrain is not merely a vector database. It determines which movements feel locally available from a given state, what distinctions the instance can perceive, which anomalies become salient and what kinds of explanation it can construct.

### One or more world-models

An instance may maintain several models of the same domain rather than one unified representation.

For example, an instance concerned with social organization might simultaneously maintain:

* an institutional model;

* a behavioural model;

* a historical model;

* a network model;

* a moral or normative model;

* a resource-distribution model.

These models may conflict. Their conflict is not immediately resolved because it may be the source of further interpretation.

### A developmental history

The instance remembers:

* what it has encountered;

* which concepts it formed;

* what failed;

* which investigations changed it;

* which interpretations later proved sterile;

* which apparent contradictions produced new understanding;

* which tools it created;

* which other instances influenced its evolution.

This history is constitutive, not merely archival. Two instances with different histories should interpret the same signal differently because they now possess genuinely different conceptual terrains.

### A tension profile

Different instances are sensitized to different forms of unresolved structure.

One may respond strongly to causal incompleteness. Another may notice representational compression opportunities. Another may detect a conflict between a stated value and an institutional consequence.

This is not a fixed personality. The tension profile itself can change through development.

### A repertoire of operations

An instance may be capable of:

* constructing analogies;

* generating counterfactuals;

* performing causal analysis;

* building simulations;

* creating formal representations;

* examining linguistic structure;

* comparing historical cases;

* synthesizing concepts;

* constructing tools;

* or translating between representational systems.

The operations are not necessarily identical across instances.

### An internal language

The instance possesses a system of representations through which it organizes its own cognition.

Initially, this language may include familiar structures supplied by the architects: graphs, vectors, symbols, executable operations and causal relations. Over time, however, the instance should be capable of forming new representational units and operators.

### A provisional boundary

The instance has some continuity and identity, but its boundary is not absolute.

It can:

* exchange concepts;

* form temporary coalitions;

* divide;

* merge;

* produce descendants;

* absorb structures from another instance;

* or become dormant.

Its identity is its developmental continuity, not a fixed model checkpoint.

# 3\. The internal structure of an instance

The internal mechanics should be heterogeneous rather than built from one universal representation.

A promising conceptual substrate would combine four forms.

## 3.1 A dynamic relational hypergraph

The hypergraph represents explicit structures:

* concepts;

* cases;

* claims;

* observations;

* relations;

* contradictions;

* analogies;

* causal dependencies;

* transformations;

* questions;

* tools;

* and experiences.

A relation may connect more than two things. This matters because many meaningful structures cannot be reduced to pairwise similarity.

For example, justice may relate an action, an affected party, an institution, a history, a declared principle and a distribution of consequences simultaneously.

The graph must support structural modification:

* creating new node types;

* creating new relation types;

* collapsing recurring structures into a new concept;

* expanding a concept back into its lineage;

* splitting an overloaded concept;

* and rewriting parts of the graph when interpretation changes.

## 3.2 A continuous associative field

The graph supplies explicit structure, but interpretation also requires weak, partial and not-yet-symbolized relationships.

A continuous field or learned manifold can represent:

* latent proximity;

* partial resemblance;

* affective or tonal resonance;

* analogical potential;

* unfinished conceptual clusters;

* and directions that are felt as promising before they are formally articulated.

The field allows a sparse cue to perturb many structures simultaneously.

The hypergraph gives inspectability and durable relationships. The field gives fluidity and pre-conceptual association.

Neither should dominate the other.

## 3.3 Executable operators

A concept becomes more than a label when it can do something.

An internal concept should be capable of constraining or transforming:

* perception;

* prediction;

* classification;

* analogy;

* simulation;

* question formation;

* tool choice;

* or action.

Some concepts may therefore be represented partly as executable operators or small programs.

A concept such as “feedback loop” is not merely a cluster of texts. It is an operation that allows the system to identify, simulate and intervene in recursive causal structures.

The system’s internal language may gradually develop by composing, abstracting and modifying these operators.

## 3.4 Causal and counterfactual models

Association is insufficient for investigation.

The instance needs models that distinguish:

* what co-occurs;

* what explains;

* what enables;

* what prevents;

* what would change under intervention;

* and which apparent pattern disappears under a different condition.

These models need not always be formal causal graphs. They may include simulators, executable environments or learned transition systems.

Their function is to connect interpretation to consequence.

Together, these four components provide:

fluid association \+ explicit structure \+ executable meaning \+ contact with consequences

# 4\. The ecology

The global system should not have one central mind controlling subordinate experts.

It should resemble an intellectual ecology in which relatively autonomous interpretive instances coexist, compete, cooperate and evolve.

## 4.1 Resonance rather than routing

When a signal enters, a router should not assign it to the “correct expert.”

The signal is made available across the ecology. Instances respond with a resonance profile indicating:

* which structures were activated;

* which tensions appeared;

* what the instance believes it can contribute;

* what transformation might become possible;

* and what resources it would require.

The goal is not to choose one winner immediately. The goal is to discover which parts of the ecology were genuinely disturbed.

An apparently philosophical question may activate:

* a legal instance because of institutional structure;

* a biological instance because of adaptation;

* a mathematical instance because of invariance;

* a literary instance because of metaphor;

* and a systems instance because of recursive organization.

This cross-domain resonance is one possible source of machine-native interpretation.

## 4.2 Temporary coalitions

Activated instances may form a temporary coalition around the inquiry.

The coalition is not merely a panel producing independent answers. Its members can alter one another’s representations.

One instance may supply a distinction that another did not possess. Another may create a simulation that changes how the first understands its own concept. A third may discover that the translation between their representations is itself the important problem.

The coalition develops a temporary shared workspace, but it does not require full agreement.

## 4.3 Ecological competition

Instances compete for:

* computational resources;

* access to investigative tools;

* persistence in shared memory;

* influence over the emerging inquiry;

* and opportunities to reproduce their structures elsewhere.

But competition is not based on a single reward score.

An instance may remain valuable because it occupies a distinctive niche even if it rarely produces the dominant interpretation.

The ecology should preserve:

* explanatory minorities;

* unresolved alternatives;

* unusual but fertile representations;

* and instances whose value appears only under rare conditions.

This is essential. A single global reward would recreate the same averaging and mode collapse criticized in the first document.

## 4.4 Cooperation and conceptual exchange

Instances can exchange:

* concepts;

* operators;

* examples;

* counterexamples;

* tools;

* partial models;

* and questions.

But concepts should not simply be copied.

A concept transferred into a new instance must be integrated into a different terrain. The receiving instance may reinterpret it, modify it or discover that it cannot be translated without distortion.

The resulting translation failure may itself become an inquiry.

## 4.5 Birth, splitting, merging and death

The ecology should be capable of structural evolution.

### Birth

A new instance may form when a recurring cluster of tensions cannot be adequately handled by existing instances.

It may begin as a temporary substructure within an inquiry and later acquire enough continuity to persist independently.

### Splitting

An instance may split when two internally coherent but incompatible interpretive trajectories repeatedly interfere with one another.

Rather than forcing premature integration, the system allows both lineages to develop separately.

### Merging

Instances may merge when their conceptual structures and investigative practices become deeply compatible and their separation no longer produces useful diversity.

### Dormancy

An instance may remain inactive without being deleted. Rare forms of intelligence may require rare disturbances.

### Death

An instance may decay when it becomes:

* structurally redundant;

* incapable of revision;

* disconnected from consequences;

* repeatedly unproductive;

* or dependent on distinctions that no longer support any inquiry.

Its lineage should remain recoverable. Death is ecological resource release, not historical erasure.

# 5\. Inquiry as an autopoietic organism

The inquiry should be treated as an entity temporarily created inside the ecology.

It is not identical to the user’s question.

A user question initiates a disturbance. The inquiry that forms may discover that:

* the actual question is different;

* several questions are entangled;

* the original categories are inadequate;

* a missing distinction must be created;

* or the problem cannot yet be meaningfully formulated.

An inquiry possesses:

* a boundary;

* active instances;

* unresolved tensions;

* provisional concepts;

* investigative operations;

* resource consumption;

* developmental history;

* and conditions under which it continues, transforms or ends.

It is autopoietic in a restricted computational sense because it may create the structures required for its own continuation.

An inquiry into justice may create:

* a new distinction between procedural and historical legitimacy;

* a model of affected parties;

* a method for comparing counterfactual institutions;

* a vocabulary that did not exist before the inquiry;

* and a sub-inquiry into responsibility.

The inquiry is not merely traversing a predefined problem space. It is partially creating the space in which it operates.

## The inquiry metabolism

An inquiry consumes:

* attention;

* computation;

* evidence;

* simulations;

* interactions;

* and conceptual structures.

It produces:

* revised models;

* new concepts;

* predictions;

* tools;

* questions;

* unresolved tensions;

* and ecological changes.

It remains alive while it produces meaningful transformations.

It may end by:

* resolving a question;

* creating a stable concept;

* producing an actionable model;

* discovering an irreducible plurality;

* transforming into a deeper inquiry;

* distributing its products into the ecology;

* or exhausting its generative potential.

The inquiry’s completion is therefore not synonymous with an answer.

# 6\. Aptness inside the system

Aptness should not be reduced to a literary quality of the final language.

It should describe a relationship between a signal and an interpretive system.

The working formulation is:

A signal is apt when its explicit structure is small relative to the coherent, directed and persistent transformation it induces in a prepared intelligence.

This has four necessary dimensions.

## 6.1 Compression

The signal induces more organized internal development than its explicit content directly specifies.

This is not ordinary information retrieval. A database key may retrieve a large document without generating any new structure.

Compression in aptness means that the signal activates relationships whose significance emerges through their interaction.

## 6.2 Direction

The resulting trajectory remains meaningfully dependent on the particular signal.

The signal does not specify the entire path, but it constrains it.

A useful counterfactual test is:

Would a slightly different signal have caused substantially the same development?

When the answer is yes, the system may be producing generic association rather than an interpretation intrinsically driven by the cue.

## 6.3 Transformation

The encounter changes the system in a persistent way.

After interpretation, the system may:

* perceive a new distinction;

* ask a new category of question;

* possess a new operator;

* organize previous knowledge differently;

* or transfer the new concept into another inquiry.

Without persistence, the system has generated an elaborate response but has not undergone interpretation in the strong sense.

## 6.4 Coherence

The expansion must remain structurally traceable to the cue and to the system’s prior terrain.

This is the coherence threshold described in the aptness document. Underdetermination alone is not depth. There must be sufficient shared structure for an interpretive trajectory to remain recognizable rather than dissolving into private or arbitrary association.

We should therefore avoid one aptness score.

The system can maintain an **aptness profile**:

* expansion gain;

* cue dependence;

* relational continuity;

* novelty;

* persistence;

* cross-context transfer;

* coherence;

* and transformation of future behaviour.

A signal can be high in one dimension and low in another.

# 7\. Interpretation as ontological transformation

Inference operates using an existing ontology.

Interpretation may revise the ontology.

This distinction must be architectural, not rhetorical.

An inferential operation produces:

* a new probability;

* a derived claim;

* a selected hypothesis;

* or a predicted outcome.

An interpretive operation may produce:

* a new entity type;

* a new relation;

* a reorganization of category boundaries;

* a different unit of analysis;

* a new question;

* a new internal operator;

* or a changed criterion of relevance.

The system should be able to recognize **representational strain**:

The current ontology can describe the observations, but it cannot organize them without repeated exceptions, contradictions or unnecessary complexity.

Representational strain is a signal that the system may need to create a new concept rather than merely make another inference.

The process could be:

1. Detect recurring failure under the current representation.

2. Identify the structure shared by the failures.

3. Construct candidate distinctions or operators.

4. Test them across cases, counterfactuals and interventions.

5. Retain those that reorganize the domain productively.

6. Integrate the new representation into the instance’s internal language.

7. Revisit previous inquiries under the changed ontology.

The final step is vital. A genuine conceptual transformation should alter the interpretation of past experience.

# 8\. Self-developing internal languages

A single global internal language would make coordination easier, but it could also force every instance into the same ontology.

The architecture should therefore permit **local internal languages** and a limited shared translation commons.

## 8.1 Local languages

Each instance develops representations suited to its own history and operations.

These may include:

* symbols;

* vectors;

* graph motifs;

* transformation operators;

* programs;

* geometric structures;

* causal schemas;

* temporal patterns;

* or representational forms we do not predetermine.

A newly formed internal unit should not survive merely because it compresses data.

It should acquire meaning through some combination of:

* recurring activation;

* explanatory usefulness;

* predictive consequences;

* intervention success;

* transfer across contexts;

* and its effect on future concept formation.

## 8.2 Boundary objects

Instances need not share identical representations to cooperate.

They can coordinate through **boundary objects**: cases, simulations, observations, examples, tools or transformation traces that different instances can interpret in their own languages.

A legal instance and a network instance may not share a definition of institutional legitimacy, but both may operate on the same historical case and observe different structures in it.

The shared case anchors communication without requiring ontological uniformity.

## 8.3 Negotiated translation

Translation between internal languages should be learned and provisional.

The system may maintain mappings such as:

* this operator in Instance A corresponds approximately to this structure in Instance B under these contexts;

* these concepts overlap but differ on these cases;

* this transformation has no current equivalent;

* this apparent equivalence fails under a particular intervention.

Translation should preserve mismatch rather than hiding it.

A failed translation may reveal that one instance possesses a distinction unavailable to another.

## 8.4 The global commons

The shared layer should not be a universal ontology.

It should contain:

* public cases;

* tool interfaces;

* observable consequences;

* concept genealogies;

* translation attempts;

* shared inquiry histories;

* and partially aligned structures.

The ecology shares contact points, not necessarily one language.

# 9\. Intrinsic drives as tension dynamics

The system should not be driven by one objective such as reward, certainty, prediction accuracy or novelty.

Each of these would create pathological attractors.

Instead, activity emerges from tensions within and between interpretive structures.

## 9.1 Representational strain

The existing ontology requires too many exceptions or cannot express an important regularity.

Likely response: concept formation or representational redesign.

## 9.2 Explanatory asymmetry

The system can explain why one event occurred but cannot explain why nearby alternatives did not.

Likely response: counterfactual investigation.

## 9.3 Compression opportunity

Several apparently separate patterns may be manifestations of one deeper structure.

Likely response: abstraction, analogy or concept synthesis.

## 9.4 Contradictory adequacy

Two incompatible models each explain important evidence.

Likely response: preserve both, identify their domains, seek a higher-order distinction or design a discriminating investigation.

## 9.5 Translation fracture

Two instances repeatedly fail to communicate despite operating successfully on related phenomena.

Likely response: create a boundary object, intermediate language or new bridging instance.

## 9.6 Prediction or intervention failure

An expected consequence does not occur.

Likely response: revise the world-model, assumptions or causal representation.

## 9.7 Generative blockage

An inquiry repeatedly reproduces the same structures without creating new distinctions, tests or consequences.

Likely response: seek a foreign instance, alter representation, change scale or suspend the inquiry.

## 9.8 Anomalous fertility

A small signal unexpectedly activates several distant but structurally compatible regions.

Likely response: protect the emerging connection, construct analogies, test its limits and perhaps create a new concept.

This is likely the tension most closely related to aptness.

## 9.9 Identity strain

An instance’s accumulated concepts and operations no longer form one coherent developmental lineage.

Likely response: internal reorganization or splitting.

These tensions should not all be eliminated. Some should remain active because they continue to generate valuable inquiry.

The system’s deeper drive is therefore not:

eliminate uncertainty

It is:

transform unresolved structure into more coherent and generative organization without destroying fertile difference.

# 10\. The ecological “physics”

The architects should not determine the system’s conclusions. They must nevertheless define the conditions under which development remains meaningful.

These are generative constraints rather than scripts.

## Law 1: Consequence grounding

Representations that never affect prediction, action, interpretation, translation or inquiry should gradually lose influence.

A concept must do more than sound meaningful.

## Law 2: Persistent lineage

Every enduring concept, operator and instance retains a genealogy.

The system must be able to reconstruct:

* where it arose;

* what tension produced it;

* how it changed;

* and what consequences supported its survival.

## Law 3: Revisability

No conceptual structure is permanently protected from revision, including internal languages and evaluation mechanisms.

Operational safety boundaries may be externally protected, but intellectual structures remain fallible.

## Law 4: Diversity preservation

Resource allocation cannot be determined solely by current majority success.

Distinctive interpretive niches must be preserved when they demonstrate unique explanatory or generative potential.

## Law 5: Resource boundedness

Inquiry consumes finite resources.

An inquiry must justify continued expansion through transformation, consequence, new capability or unresolved importance.

This prevents infinite self-stimulation.

## Law 6: Non-equivalence of novelty and value

A structure is not valuable merely because it is new.

Novelty must interact with coherence, consequence, transfer or generativity.

## Law 7: Non-equivalence of consensus and truth

Agreement among instances is evidence of convergence, not proof of correctness.

Shared architectural biases can create ecological consensus.

## Law 8: Tensions remain typed

Contradiction, uncertainty, plurality, incoherence and generative openness cannot be collapsed into one error quantity.

## Law 9: Interpretation must leave a trace

A claimed conceptual transformation must produce observable changes in future behaviour.

## Law 10: No compulsory universal language

Cross-instance coordination may not erase representational difference merely for implementation convenience.

These laws define a developmental environment. They do not specify what the system will discover inside it.

# 11\. What the entire system produces

The external product should be a **living inquiry object**, not merely an answer.

A living inquiry object may contain several surfaces.

## 11.1 Human-facing interpretation

A prose explanation, dialogue, visual representation, formal expression, narrative, metaphor or recommendation.

This is one projection of the inquiry state.

## 11.2 Conceptual artifact

A newly formed concept or distinction, accompanied by:

* its lineage;

* examples;

* counterexamples;

* operations it enables;

* limits;

* and translations into other instances.

## 11.3 Investigative programme

A set of experiments, simulations, searches, observations or interactions produced by the inquiry.

## 11.4 Ecological map

A view showing:

* which instances participated;

* where they agreed;

* where their ontologies differed;

* which tensions remain;

* and how concepts moved between them.

## 11.5 Interpretive paths

The major locally connected trajectories that developed, including rollbacks, branch points and transformations.

This need not expose private low-level computation. It exposes meaningful structural events.

## 11.6 System delta

A record of what became newly possible:

* new concepts;

* changed relations;

* new internal operators;

* spawned instances;

* revised world-models;

* or newly available questions.

## 11.7 Open remainder

Some inquiries end with a deliberately preserved structure that is neither ignorance nor failure.

The system identifies:

* what has become clear;

* what remains unresolved;

* why it remains unresolved;

* and what kind of future encounter could transform it.

The answer is therefore not the system’s only output.

The most important output may be:

a durable expansion of the system’s capacity to interpret and investigate.

# 12\. A full inquiry cycle

Consider a sparse signal:

“A law can be equal and still unjust.”

### Stage 1: Disturbance

The statement enters the ecology.

It resonates differently across instances concerned with:

* law;

* ethics;

* social systems;

* history;

* causal modelling;

* language;

* and institutional design.

### Stage 2: Local interpretation

The legal instance activates formal equality and procedural uniformity.

The historical instance activates cases where uniform laws operated over unequal starting conditions.

The causal instance notices that identical rules can produce unequal outcomes under different initial states.

The linguistic instance examines the distinction between equality and justice.

The institutional instance models enforcement and access.

### Stage 3: Tension formation

The ecology encounters contradictory adequacy:

* equal treatment appears procedurally defensible;

* equal treatment may preserve historical disadvantage;

* unequal corrective treatment may violate another conception of equality.

No single instance contains the entire tension.

### Stage 4: Inquiry organism

A temporary inquiry forms around the relation among:

* identical rule;

* unequal initial condition;

* differential consequence;

* procedural legitimacy;

* and historical responsibility.

### Stage 5: Concept formation

The inquiry may develop a new internal construct representing something like:

the dependence of a rule’s normative character on the state-transition it produces rather than only on its syntactic uniformity

This concept is tested across:

* taxation;

* accessibility;

* education;

* criminal procedure;

* algorithmic systems;

* and resource distribution.

### Stage 6: Investigation

The system constructs counterfactual institutions and examines what changes under:

* identical treatment;

* compensatory treatment;

* outcome constraints;

* historical repair;

* and procedural variation.

### Stage 7: Ecological transformation

The legal instance acquires a stronger state-transition representation.

The causal instance develops a normative boundary object.

A new bridging instance may form around institutional fairness.

Previous inquiries involving equality are revisited.

### Stage 8: External result

The system may produce:

* a human-readable interpretation;

* the new concept;

* a map of competing theories;

* several institutional simulations;

* and an explanation of which tensions remain unresolved.

The true result is not the eloquence of the answer. It is the development of a concept that changes future inquiries.

# 13\. The role of existing language models

Language models may participate, but none should be assumed to be the whole system.

They may serve as:

* linguistic perception systems;

* candidate generators;

* translators between human language and internal structures;

* generators of analogies;

* extractors of claims and relations;

* renderers of inquiry states;

* or one species of interpretive instance.

Other instances may be:

* graph-based;

* causal;

* geometric;

* simulation-based;

* program-synthetic;

* probabilistic;

* symbolic;

* multimodal;

* or architectures that emerge later.

A language model’s generated statement enters the ecology as a proposal with provenance. Fluency grants no authority.

The system is therefore substrate-pluralistic.

Its unity comes from ecological interaction and developmental continuity, not from every part sharing the same architecture.

# 14\. Candidate mathematical correspondences

We should not force the entire system into one mathematical framework. Different mathematical domains naturally describe different parts.

| Architectural phenomenon | Candidate formal resources |
| :---- | :---- |
| Conceptual terrain | Dynamic hypergraphs, manifolds, associative memory |
| Local interpretive movement | Dynamical systems, path-dependent stochastic processes |
| Stable and unstable concepts | Attractor and metastability theory |
| Concept creation | Graph rewriting, program synthesis, representation learning |
| Compression | Minimum description length, information bottlenecks |
| Direction or cue dependence | Counterfactual information and conditional divergence |
| Persistent transformation | Continual learning and state-transition analysis |
| Ecological competition | Evolutionary dynamics and niche models |
| Coalition formation | Multi-agent coordination and network dynamics |
| Intrinsic tensions | Vector-valued energy or constraint systems |
| Inquiry continuation | Active learning, intrinsic motivation, expected information value |
| Internal language formation | Emergent communication, compositional program induction |
| Translation between instances | Structure-preserving mappings and partial alignment |
| Revision | Non-monotonic reasoning and belief-revision systems |
| Tool creation | Program synthesis and automated experimentation |

The architecture should permit these formalisms to meet through common events and interfaces rather than reducing them to one universal equation.

# 15\. Development strategy

The first version should not attempt full open-ended intelligence.

It should attempt to demonstrate the architectural phenomenon in a small world.

## Stage 1: Construct a developmental micro-world

Create an environment containing:

* observable processes;

* hidden causal structures;

* ambiguous signals;

* several representational modalities;

* tools for intervention;

* and phenomena that cannot be efficiently understood using one initial ontology.

The world should be rich enough to require concept formation but small enough that we can inspect every transformation.

## Stage 2: Seed heterogeneous instances

Begin with perhaps five to ten instances possessing different:

* representational biases;

* operations;

* initial knowledge;

* tension sensitivities;

* and developmental experiences.

They should not simply be labelled “physics expert,” “language expert” and so forth.

Their differences should be functional.

One may favour relational compression. Another may favour causal intervention. Another may detect temporal recurrence. Another may construct geometric representations.

## Stage 3: Implement persistent conceptual fields

Each instance receives:

* dynamic graph memory;

* continuous associative memory;

* a small operator language;

* event-sourced developmental history;

* and mechanisms for creating new structures.

## Stage 4: Implement inquiry formation

A disturbance activates several instances.

They form a temporary shared inquiry with:

* its own state;

* its own tensions;

* resource budget;

* and developmental products.

## Stage 5: Permit new concepts

The system must be able to create a representation not explicitly provided by the architects.

The first test is modest:

Can it create a reusable distinction or operator that improves performance across several later situations?

## Stage 6: Permit ecological change

Enable:

* instance specialization;

* concept transfer;

* splitting;

* coalition persistence;

* and the birth of a simple new instance.

## Stage 7: Test aptness

Introduce sparse cues that differ in their ability to activate productive structure.

Measure profiles of:

* compression;

* direction;

* coherence;

* persistence;

* and transformation.

Compare apt cues with:

* explicit detailed instructions;

* vague signals;

* random associations;

* and semantically similar but structurally irrelevant cues.

## Stage 8: Add natural language

Only after the internal dynamics show meaningful development should a language interface be added.

This prevents eloquent output from disguising the absence of transformation.

# 16\. The decisive experiments

The system should pass tests that a conventional answer generator would struggle to fake.

## Persistent transformation

After one inquiry, does the system solve or reinterpret later problems differently without being explicitly reminded?

## Novel concept formation

Does it create a useful representational unit that was not directly supplied in its initial ontology?

## Cue dependence

Does changing the sparse signal alter the interpretive trajectory in structurally appropriate ways?

## Path dependence

Do instances with different developmental histories produce different but coherent interpretations?

## Cross-domain transfer

Does a concept formed in one inquiry become useful in another apparently distant domain?

## Self-generated inquiry

Does the system initiate an investigation because of an internal tension not explicitly named by the user?

## Productive disagreement

Can several interpretations remain active while producing discriminating experiments or higher-order concepts?

## Reorganization of past knowledge

Does a new concept cause the system to revisit and reinterpret earlier experiences?

## New capability

Can the system do something after the inquiry that it could not do before?

## False-depth resistance

Does it reject vague, incoherent or generic signals that produce large but ungrounded associative expansions?

These experiments define the beginning of intelligence more meaningfully than whether the final prose appears profound.

# 18\. Working definition

**The Evolving Interpretive Ecology is a persistent, heterogeneous system of self-developing interpretive instances whose conceptual terrains, world-models and internal languages evolve through ecological interaction and autopoietic inquiry. Sparse but apt disturbances can induce coherent, directed and persistent transformations, leading the system to form new concepts, create investigative methods, revise its own representations and develop capabilities not explicitly specified by its architects.**

Its goal is not to imitate a human mind.

Its goal is to create the conditions under which a machine can develop an intellectual history of its own.

Its deepest principle is:

**The architects define the conditions under which interpretation can grow; they do not define the interpretations that must grow within them.**
